ABSTRACT

High-mass X-ray binaries are one of the primary drivers of the 21-cm signal from Cosmic Dawn and Reionization, playing
a leading role in the thermal history of the intergalactic medium. In traditional semi-numerical simulations, a deterministic
scaling relation between the total X-ray luminosity of high-mass X-ray binaries, 𝐿 X , and star formation rate (SFR) is usually
adopted. However, this assumption is inaccurate for high-redshift low-SFR regions hosting few sources. The spatial variation
in the number and luminosity of these sources is expected to enhance fluctuations in the Cosmic Dawn 21-cm signal. Here we
quantify this effect by introducing a stochastic 𝐿 X model sampled from a power-law X-ray luminosity function. Implementing
this in 21cmSPACE, a large-scale simulation framework of Cosmic Dawn and Reionization, we find that the stochasticity leads
to enhanced fluctuations in X-ray heating rate fields, and affects the 21-cm power spectrum on small scales (𝑘 > 0.3 cMpc−1 ).
The impact of stochasticity on the global 21-cm signal and on the large-scale power spectrum is found to be negligible. Our
results suggest these effects will remain undetected by the upcoming Square Kilometer Array. However, large-scale lunar-based
experiments may be sensitive to the signatures of stochastic X-ray heating at 𝑧 ∼ 25. Quantifying these corrections is a vital step
toward robust 21-cm modeling and ensuring that future precision data interpretation is free from astrophysical biases.

1 INTRODUCTION
The 21-cm line of neutral hydrogen is expected to be one of the most
powerful probes of the early Universe, offering a unique window onto
the earliest epochs of cosmic evolution, including the Dark Ages
(DA), Cosmic Dawn (CD), and the Epoch of Reionization (EoR)
(e.g. Madau et al. 1997; Furlanetto et al. 2006). Radiation from
early sources such as the first generations of stars, i.e. Population
III (Pop III) stars, and the subsequent population of X-ray binaries
(XRBs) affects the hydrogen signal (Wouthuysen 1952; Field 1958;
Chen & Miralda-Escudé 2004; Fragos et al. 2013b; Pacucci et al.
2014; Fialkov et al. 2014; Madau & Fragos 2017; Eide et al. 2018;
Fialkov et al. 2017a; Ross et al. 2017; Mebane et al. 2020; GesseyJones et al. 2022; Hegde & Furlanetto 2023; Sartorio et al. 2023;
Ventura et al. 2023; Gessey-Jones et al. 2025), thereby imprinting
their signatures onto the high-redshift observable. This dependence
makes the 21-cm signal one of the best probes of the high-redshift
heating and ionization of the intergalactic medium (IGM).

The immense scientific promise of the 21-cm signal in unveiling
the early Universe has motivated extensive observational efforts with
low-frequency radio telescopes. Currently, observations concentrate
on two summary statistics: the sky-averaged global signal targeted by
instruments such as EDGES (Bowman et al. 2018), SARAS (Singh
et al. 2022), REACH (de Lera Acedo et al. 2022), MIST (Monsalve
et al. 2024), RHINO (Bull et al. 2024a), GINAN (McKay et al.
2025) and PRIZ M (Philip et al. 2019) among others, and the power
spectrum of spatial fluctuations observed by interferometric arrays
such as MWA (Tingay et al. 2013), LOFAR (van Haarlem et al.
2013), NenuFAR (Mertens et al. 2021; Munshi et al. 2025) and
HERA (DeBoer & HERA 2015). Future experiments might also
attempt to construct three-dimensional tomographic maps of the 21cm signal (e.g. the under-construction Square Kilometer Array, SKA
Koopmans et al. 2015), and extend the measurements into the cosmic
DA using moon-based telescopes (see, e.g. Bale et al. 2023; Burns
& Hallinan 2020; Burns et al. 2022; Goel et al. 2022; Polidan et al.
2024; Klein Wolt et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2021; Fialkov et al. 2024;
Artuc & de Lera Acedo 2024; Brinkerink et al. 2025).
Despite the dedicated observational effort, the detection of the
cosmological 21-cm signal remains extremely challenging due to

its faint nature, particularly in comparison to the bright Galactic and
extragalactic foregrounds (Shaver et al. 1999; Bharadwaj & Ali 2005;
Jelić et al. 2008, 2010; Datta et al. 2010). Additional hurdles include
the telescope beam modeling (Kim et al. 2022; Dasgupta et al. 2023),
instrumental noise (Nasirudin et al. 2020; Pal et al. 2025), the effect
of the ionosphere (Datta et al. 2016; Shen et al. 2021), calibration

A key advance in the field came from the EDGES Low-Band experiment, which detected a tentative cosmological signal—a deep
absorption feature centered at a redshift of 𝑧 ∼ 17 (Bowman et al.
2018). The following SARAS 3 attempt to verify this signal resulted
in the best-fit EDGES profile being rejected with 95.5% confidence
(Singh et al. 2022). In addition to the observational discrepancy,
the best-fit EDGES Low-Band absorption profile is too deep to be
explained by conventional astrophysical modelling, requiring exotic
astrophysical scenarios beyond the ΛCDM paradigm (e.g. Barkana
2018; Berlin et al. 2018; Feng & Holder 2018; Fialkov et al. 2018;
Hill & Baxter 2018; Muñoz & Loeb 2018; Brandenberger et al.
2019; Liu et al. 2019; Mittal et al. 2022). However, it is not yet clear
whether the EDGES Low-Band signal is truly cosmological, with
studies such as Hills et al. (2018); Singh & Subrahmanyan (2019);
Bradley et al. (2019); Sims & Pober (2020) questioning its origin
and suggesting systematic uncertainties as a potential source of the
features in the data. The quest for experimental verification continues with other global experiments, including SARAS 3, REACH,
MIST and GINAN; while interferometers (e.g. Gehlot et al. 2020)
are searching for the corresponding enhancement in the power spectrum (e.g. Fialkov & Barkana 2019; Reis et al. 2020). While a robust
detection remains elusive, the 21-cm signal parameter space is being
systematically narrowed by increasingly stringent upper limits from
current experiments (Abdurashidova et al. 2022; Ghara et al. 2025;
Trott et al. 2025), alongside growing synergies with JWST and other
high-redshift probes (Dhandha et al. 2025a; Sims et al. 2025). Looking forward, the SKA, currently under construction, is designed to
detect the 21-cm signal and map its evolution across a vast range of
cosmic redshifts and spatial scales.
The committed observational effort requires a matched theoretical
development of accurate signal models. The feedback exerted by the
first sources on the IGM during CD significantly affects the 21-cm
signal. This enables constraints on different properties of Pop III
stars like their formation history, initial mass function, X-ray and UV
emission efficiencies (e.g. Fialkov et al. 2013; Fialkov & Barkana
2014; Fialkov 2018; Madau 2018; Ma et al. 2018; Schauer et al. 2019;
Mebane et al. 2020; Gessey-Jones et al. 2022, 2025; Ventura et al.
2023, 2025; Lazare et al. 2024; Pochinda et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025;
Wasserman et al. 2025). For instance, recent 21-cm observations from
HERA and SARAS 3 constrain the average star formation efficiency
of Pop III stars to be below 5.5% at 68% confidence (Pochinda
et al. 2024). It has been shown by Gessey-Jones et al. (2025) that
future observations achieving a 25 mK sensitivity measurement of
the global 21-cm signal can provide 3𝜎 constraints on the initial
mass function of Pop III stars owing to its strong correlation with the
X-ray efficiency (Sartorio et al. 2023) and Lyman-band luminosity
(Gessey-Jones et al. 2022). Besides, Dhandha et al. (2025b) found
that the average X-ray efficiency of high-redshift (𝑧 > 6) galaxies
(including Pop III galaxies) can be constrained with ∼ 0.7 dex errors
at 68% confidence levels by exploiting synergies between JWST and
21-cm observations, with the total X-ray luminosity, 𝐿 𝑋 , per star
formation rate (SFR), 𝑓 𝑋 = 𝐿 𝑋 /SFR, constrained to 𝑓 𝑋 = 0.8+9.7
−0.4 .

The existing analysis and parameter inference rely on the deterministic linear scaling relations between SFR and 𝐿 𝑋 based on local
observations (e.g. Grimm et al. 2003a; Antoniou & Zezas 2016) and
binary population synthesis (BPS) studies (e.g. Fragos et al. 2013a;
Sartorio et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024) of XRBs. These studies include
high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs) in low-metallicity regions, which
act as proxies for high-redshift XRB populations (Brorby et al. 2016)
and inform 21-cm modeling (e.g. Pacucci et al. 2014; Fialkov et al.
2014; Ross et al. 2017; Kaur et al. 2022). However, this deterministic approach is accurate only when applied to large samples of
XRBs, while the total luminosities of a small cluster of XRBs can
show significant scatter around the mean luminosity predicted by the
𝐿 𝑋 −SFR scaling relation.
In reality, HMXBs are discrete, short-lived objects, and their luminosity and number density depend on the environment (e.g. Mineo
et al. 2012; Gilfanov et al. 2022). The observed luminosity function
of HMXBs in the local Universe can be approximated as a power law,
i.e. 𝑑𝑁/𝑑𝐿 ∝ 𝐿 − 𝛼 , where 𝛼 ∼ 1.6 (Gilfanov et al. 2022), meaning
that most X-rays come from a small number of luminous objects1 . The
bright HMXBs become more dominant in more metal-poor regions
(Lehmer et al. 2021, see their fig. 3). Thus, we expect a stochastic
relation described by an X-ray Luminosity Function (XLF) to be a
better representation of the CD XRB population than the deterministic 𝐿 𝑋 −SFR relation.
To provide a more realistic description of the thermal history of the
IGM, we incorporate a stochastic XLF into the large-scale cosmological semi-numerical framework 21cmSPACE (e.g. Visbal et al. 2012;
Fialkov et al. 2013; Gessey-Jones et al. 2025), which models CD
and EoR observable signals, including the 21-cm signal of neutral
hydrogen, JWST UVLFs, as well as X-ray and radio backgrounds.
While previous work has examined stochasticity in star formation efficiency, photon propagation, and star formation scatter—primarily
during the EoR (e.g. Reis et al. 2022; Nikolić et al. 2024; Murmu
et al. 2024); our study is the first to quantify the impact of the XLF
itself on the 21-cm signal.
In this work, we develop a novel stochastic model to generate 𝐿 X
from the XLF and SFR across the simulation box, effectively capturing the discrete and localized nature of the first sources of heat:
Population III XRBs2 . By introducing these stochastic sources into
our simulations, we account for significant spatial fluctuations in the
IGM temperature—particularly in low-SFR regions—that are not
captured by traditional deterministic scaling relations. We discuss
how variations in the shape and normalization of the Pop III XLF
affect 21-cm observables, contributing to a robust theoretical framework for interpreting next-generation observations with the SKA and
upcoming lunar-based missions.

2 METHODOLOGY
The intensity and fluctuations of the 21-cm signal are influenced
by both cosmological and astrophysical phenomena (e.g. Furlanetto
et al. 2006). One critical astrophysical process is the injection of energy into the IGM by X-rays. XRBs, particularly black-hole binaries,
are believed to be the dominant sources of X-ray photons during CD
and the EoR, at 𝑧 ≈ 6 − 20 (e.g. Fragos et al. 2013b), and are widely
recognized as key players in shaping the thermal evolution of the
Universe throughout these epochs (Fragos et al. 2013b; Fialkov et al.
2014; Eide et al. 2018; Gilfanov et al. 2022; Sartorio et al. 2023).
In this section, we develop the methodology, first discussing the
stochastic modeling of XRB luminosities in subsection 2.1. We then
test the importance of XRB stochasticity and its implications for CDEoR observables by introducing this prescription into 21cmSPACE in
subsection 2.2.
2.1 Stochastic modeling of XRB luminosities
Previous theoretical works have suggested that the nature of XRBs
in high-redshift environments, such as those containing Pop III stars
(Sartorio et al. 2023), differs from more metal-rich stellar populations
(e.g. Fragos et al. 2013a,b; Liu et al. 2024). These Pop III stars
are thought to have higher X-ray luminosity per star formation rate
than their metal-rich counterparts, which can lead to significant Xray feedback on the IGM, influencing both heating and ionization
(Fialkov et al. 2017b). During the onset of CD, characterized by
the small-number statistics of XRBs, it might become important to
model the stochastic Pop III X-ray luminosity, rather than assuming
a deterministic 𝐿 𝑋 −SFR scaling relation. We provide a summary of
all the symbols used for stochastic XLB modeling in Table 1.

2.2 Implementation in 21cmSPACE
A key ingredient in the 21-cm signal modelling is an accurate treatment of the thermal and ionization state of the intergalactic gas
strongly affected by heating sources such as XRBs (Pritchard &
Furlanetto 2007; Mesinger et al. 2011; Fialkov et al. 2014). To model
the impact of stochastic XRBs on the 21-cm signal, we implement
the stochastic XLF approach discussed in Sec. 2.1 into 21cmSPACE
(Fialkov et al. 2012; Visbal et al. 2012)4 , which models a universe
across the cosmic DA, CD and EoR.
The simulations are performed in a box of (384 cMpc) 3 resolved
into 1283 cells, with a cell size of 3 cMpc. The simulations are
initialized at redshift 𝑧 = 50 with initial conditions for density and
velocity fields generated using power spectra from CAMB (Lewis &
Challinor 2011). The evolution of baryons and dark matter density
fields on scales larger than the simulation cell is followed using
linear theory. On smaller (sub-grid) scales, halo formation and the
subsequent gas collapse are addressed using a hybrid halo mass
function (Barkana & Loeb 2005b; Tseliakhovich et al. 2011), and
accounting for suppression due to streaming velocities (Tseliakhovich
& Hirata 2010; Fialkov et al. 2012). A fraction 𝑓★ of the collapsed
gas in halos above the threshold circular velocity 𝑉𝑐 is assumed to
form stars.
21cmSPACE adopts a star formation model of the primordial Pop III
and the metal-enriched Pop II stars with Pop III stellar spectra calculated using an in-house model (Gessey-Jones et al. 2022), assuming
a log-flat Initial Mass Function (IMF) described by a power-law with
a slop 𝛼III for stars of masses ∈ [𝑀min,III , 𝑀max,III ] and accounting for the impact of Lyman-Werner radiation and relative velocity
between dark matter and gas on star formation efficiency (Fialkov
et al. 2013). The Pop II spectra are calculated using Starburst99
(Leitherer et al. 1999) as described in Barkana & Loeb (2005a). The
transition between Pop III and Pop II star formation occurs with a
delay 𝑡 recov , which mimics the impact of supernovae on the recollapse
of the star-forming gas Magg et al. (2022). Pop III and Pop II XRBs
trace corresponding SFRs and have X-ray efficiencies of 𝑓 𝑋,III and
𝑓 𝑋,II , respectively. In this work, XRB stochasticity is implemented
exclusively for Pop III stars where low- 𝑁ˆ effects are most dominant
(see further discussion in Appendix A2). For simplicity, we fix 𝑓 𝑋,II
at its fiducial value of 1 and hereafter denote the Pop III X-ray efficiency 𝑓 𝑋,III simply as 𝑓 𝑋 . Furthermore, the resulting populations of
XRBs are assumed to have a power-law spectral energy distributions
(SED) with a spectral index 𝛽 = 1.5 and a minimum energy cutoff of
𝐸 min = 0.1 keV, for both Pop II and Pop III XRBs. Star formation at
late times is susceptible to the photoheating feedback (Madau et al.
1997; Barkana & Loeb 2004; Sobacchi & Mesinger 2013; Cohen
et al. 2016). Although we focus on the high-redshift regime of CD,
for completeness, we include reionization implemented using the
excursion-set formalism (Furlanetto et al. 2004) with the ionizing efficiency 𝜁 and assuming the mean free path of ionizing photons 𝑅mfp .

3.1 Redshift evolution of X-ray heating rate
As we alluded to in Sec. 2.2, X-ray emissivity (Eq. 4) and the resulting
X-ray heating rate (Eq. 6) are the key quantities that encode the XRB
stochasticity. Figure 5 shows the evolution of the X-ray heating rate
per baryon with redshift (columns) and for different values of the
stochasticity index 𝛼 (rows). All of the simulations were run with the
same initial conditions and assuming astrophysical parameters from
Table 2 with 𝑓 𝑋 = 1.
Due to the finite lifetime of Pop III X-ray binaries and the inherent
stochasticity in their formation, the X-ray luminosity of each simulation cell exhibits temporal fluctuations. These fluctuations have
a characteristic correlation timescale set by the typical lifetime of
Pop III X-ray binaries. As the X-ray photons propagate outward from
their production sites, temporal variations in the local luminosity
are manifested as spatial modulations in the heating rate per baryon,
which are visible in Figure 5. At high redshifts (𝑧 ≳ 25), the impact
of stochasticity is more pronounced as very few luminous XRBs appear, and there are “dark" gaps in the X-ray emissivity fields between
the death of one source and the appearance of another. The corresponding heating rate fields inherit this behavior which is manifested
as concentric shell-like structures around star-forming regions. The
width of the shells reflects the typical lifetime of the X-ray binaries
(10 Myrs in our case). The intensity of the ring-like features are sensitive to the stochasticity parameter 𝛼 of the Pop III XRBs. Models
with smaller 𝛼 exhibit stronger stochasticity, which leads to fewer but
more luminous XRBs and correspondingly more pronounced shelllike heating structures, whereas larger values of 𝛼 are more similar
to the deterministic case (top row in Figure 5).
The heating rate is also affected by our choice of the X-ray SEDs.
We have selected a relatively soft X-ray SED with spectral index
𝛽 = 1.5 and minimum energy cutoff of 𝐸 min = 0.1 keV which
highlights the stochasticity. For this SED, 50% of energy is emitted
in photons with 𝐸 ≲ 0.375 keV and mean free paths ≲ 3.2 Mpc
(Pritchard & Furlanetto 2007). In other words, approximately half of
the X-ray energy is injected on sub-grid scales, contributing to the
temperature of the host cell. This explains the hot red pixels observed
on some of the maps in the centres of the concentric rings and heated
bubbles.
As redshift decreases, the star formation rate increases, leading
to a more abundant population of X-ray sources with no significant
gaps in X-ray emissivity, which explains the gradual disappearance
of the shell-like features in the heating rate field. This transition is
facilitated by the shift in the stellar population from Pop III to Pop II
at around 𝑧 ∼ 23 for the selected astrophysical parameters6 , beyond
which Pop II XRBs dominate the X-ray emissivity and stochastic
effects are expected to be small. We refer the readers to Appendix
A2 for more details about the roles of Pop II XRBs.
At low redshifts, we expect the stochastic and deterministic cases
to converge fully. This trend is visible at the low-redshift end (last
column) of Figure 5 where we show the results for 𝑧 = 16.

4.2 Prospects for Lunar-Based Observatories
Given the limitations of SKA1-Low in accessing the high wavenumber regime (𝑘 > 0.3 cMpc−1 at 𝑧 ∼ 25), lunar-based observatories
may offer a promising path forward enabling CD and DA science
(Mondal & Barkana 2023; Fialkov et al. 2024). The radio-quiet far
side of the Moon provides a pristine environment for low frequency
radio astronomy, free from terrestrial radio frequency interference
and ionospheric distortions. Despite the premature termination of the
NASA’s ROLSES-1 mission due to a hard landing, the experiment
returned valuable power spectral density measurements, demonstrating the feasibility of radio observations from the lunar surface (Burns
et al. 2021). Future missions like ROLSES-2 (Hibbard et al. 2025)

and LuSEE-Night (Bale et al. 2023) are expected to provide first-oftheir-kind measurements of the low-frequency sky, including constraints on the global 21-cm signal (Hibbard et al. 2025). Ambitious
proposals, such as FarView (Polidan et al. 2024), DSL/HONGMENG
(Chen et al. 2021), PRATUSH (Sathyanarayana Rao et al. 2023) and
DEX/ALO (Brinkerink et al. 2025) among others, will offer a promising way towards probing the small-scale structure in the CD 21-cm
signal that are crucial for detecting the imprint of the stochastic

5 CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we have explored the impact of stochastic X-ray heating
from X-ray binaries (XRBs), particularly those arising from Population III (Pop III) stars on the redshifted 21-cm signal from Cosmic
Dawn. Traditional semi-numerical simulations of Cosmic Dawn and
the Epoch of Reionization often assume a fixed, deterministic scaling
between star formation rate (SFR) and X-ray luminosity. However,
this simplification fails to capture the discrete and short-lived nature
of X-ray sources in the early Universe, especially in low-mass, lowSFR environments where small-number statistics dominate. In such
regimes, the presence or absence of even a single bright source can
significantly influence the local thermal and ionization history of the
intergalactic medium (IGM), calling for a stochastic treatment.
To address this, we model the X-ray luminosity of a source as
a stochastic variable, sampling from a power-law X-ray luminosity
function (XLF) with slope 𝛼 and treating the number of XRBs per
simulation cell as a Poisson-distributed quantity with mean 𝑁ˆ that
is determined by SFR, X-ray efficiency 𝑓 𝑋 and average luminosity
per XRB ⟨L⟩ (determined by the XLF, see Eq. 2). The total luminosity in each cell is then computed via Monte Carlo realizations
of individual XRB luminosities, normalized by the expected mean
luminosity 𝐿ˆ 𝑋 , yielding a dimensionless variable 𝑙 = 𝐿 𝑋 / 𝐿ˆ 𝑋 . This
sampling procedure reproduces the expected statistical behavior of
XRB populations across different stellar environments, and is validated against binary population synthesis (BPS) results (Liu et al.
2024). The resulting distribution of 𝑙 is used during the simulation
runtime to draw stochastic luminosities on a cell-by-cell basis within
the 21cmSPACE simulation framework.
Our simulations reveal that while the stochasticity has a negligible
impact on the thermal history of the universe, the global 21-cm signal
and the large-scale power spectrum at 𝑘 < 0.3 cMpc−1 , it enhances
power at smaller scales over a broad redshift range 𝑧 ∼ 15−35. Owing
to the scarcity of sources at Cosmic Dawn, the effects are most salient
at 𝑧 ≳ 25, where we find an enhancement of up to 100% at 𝑘 = 1
cMpc−1 with 𝛼 = 0.2 and a Pop III XRB efficiency of 𝑓 𝑋 = 100,
motivated by recent BPS simulations with a log-flat Pop III stellar
initial mass function (Sartorio et al. 2023). The stochastic signatures
gradually fade away at lower redshifts as source abundance increases.
The impact of stochasticity is strongest when the XLF slope (𝛼) is
shallow. In contrast, high values of 𝛼 result in X-ray heating fields
more similar to the deterministic case.
Despite these theoretical discrepancies between stochastic and deterministic models, we find that existing and upcoming ground-based
interferometers such as SKA1-Low may not be able to discriminate between stochastic and deterministic X-ray heating models due
to their limited sensitivity and high thermal noise at small scales,
where the impact of the stochasticity is mostly pronounced. However, some of the proposed lunar-based radio observatories may offer
a viable path to accessing the high-resolution observations at low
MNRAS 000, 1–17 (2026)

radio frequencies required to detect the subtle features imprinted by
stochastic heating. Our results underscore the importance of modeling the stochasticity of the first sources and its impact on the 21-cm
signal in the range of redshifts (𝑧 ≳ 20) and scales (𝑘 > 0.5 cMpc−1 )
accessible from the Moon. Incorporating stochastic behavior into
simulations will be essential for an unbiased interpretation of forthcoming lunar and space observations.

This supports our assumption that Pop II XRBs, though they significantly contribute to global heating at lower redshifts, hardly affect the
stochasticity-driven variance at high redshifts (𝑧 ≳ 16) in the regime
of early X-ray heating that we are concerned with here. In our case,
this early heating is dominated by Pop III XRBs given their enhanced
efficiency 𝑓 𝑋 = 100 motivated by recent BPS simulations (Sartorio
et al. 2023). If 𝑓 𝑋 is smaller, as in a case of either the bottom-heavy
Salpeter-like Pop III stellar IMF or an extremely top-heavy IMF
(Gessey-Jones et al. 2025), the epoch of heating is delayed, and/or

instead driven by Pop II XRBs. In this case, the stochastic effects are
expected to be weaker and shifted to lower redshifts, as the global
formation rate of Pop II stars increases more rapidly over time than
the Pop III case, leading to faster late-time convergence to the deterministic case with rapidly increasing numbers of XRBs. A detailed
investigation of the effects of Pop II stochastic XRBs is deferred to
future work.
