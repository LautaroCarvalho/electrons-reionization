ABSTRACT

The sources responsible for the Epoch of Reionization (EoR), the last major phase transition of the Universe, remain highly elusive.
While JWST has begun to illuminate the properties of early galaxies, the direct detection of their ionizing photons is virtually impossible due to the neutral inter-galactic medium (IGM) at z ≳ 6. A direct estimate of the escape fraction of ionizing photons fesc is
thus not possible. However, the escape fraction is encoded in spectral features that trace a low opacity to ionizing photons, namely the
reduction of nebular emission with increasing fesc . Here, we exploit the large archive of JWST/NIRSpec spectra at 5 < zspec < 10 from
the DAWN JWST Archive to provide new constraints on the ionizing emissivity of EoR galaxies and on the timeline of reionization.
Through detailed spectral fitting of 1428 galaxies with a picket-fence model for the escape of ionizing photons, we derive posteriors
of fesc together with the effective ionizing output. This approach yields the escaping ionizing output of each galaxy while bypassing the usual decomposition into the ionizing photon production efficiency (ξion ) and fesc . Combining these measurements with UV
luminosity functions (LFs), we derive the hydrogen ionizing LFs, resulting in an observationally-derived ionizing photon budget at
5 < z < 15, integrated down to MUV = −13. Under standard assumptions, our budget yields an evolution of the IGM neutral fraction
consistent with independent probes, with reionization ending at z ∼ 5.8. Given the evolving shape of the ionizing LF, bright sources
(MUV ∼ −20) dominate the cosmic ionizing emissivity ṅion at lower redshift (z = 5 − 6), whereas at z > 6 faint galaxies (MUV > −18)
contribute roughly equally. Remarkably, a mere ∼20% of sources, those with fesc > 10%, produce ∼ 87% of the ionizing photons.
Overall, our results support a picture in which a few strongly leaking galaxies drive most of reionization.
Key words. galaxies: high-redshift – cosmology: dark ages, reionization, first stars – early Universe

1. Introduction
The epoch of reionization (EoR) is the last major phase transition of the Universe, when the hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM) transitioned from completely neutral to
completely ionized (Becker et al. 2015; Dayal & Ferrara 2018;
Robertson 2022; Fan et al. 2023; Ellis 2025; Stark et al. 2026,
for some recent reviews), finally allowing Lyman Continuum (LyC) photons (λrest < 912 Å) to travel through it
mostly unimpeded. This process started with the formation
of the first luminous objects (Barkana & Loeb 2001) and was
likely driven by star-forming galaxies (e.g. Bouwens et al. 2015;
Robertson et al. 2015; Dayal et al. 2025). However, there is
still debate about whether faint (Atek et al. 2024) or bright
(Naidu et al. 2020) galaxies dominate this transition. Active
Galactic Nuclei (AGNs) have also been suggested as important contributors to this process (Giallongo et al. 2015;
Madau & Haardt 2015; Grazian et al. 2024; Madau et al. 2024),
although studies on their luminosity function in the EoR suggest
that their contribution is likely minor, compared to that of galaxies (Mitra et al. 2018; Hassan et al. 2018; Kulkarni et al. 2019;
Jiang et al. 2022; Matsuoka et al. 2023).

In this context, the process of reionization is expected to be
very patchy (e.g., D’Aloisio et al. 2015; Jamieson et al. 2025),
with bubbles of ionized gas forming and growing around the objects that leak the most ionizing photons. This is seen directly
in observations of the Gunn-Peterson trough, which show fieldto-field variations towards the end of the EoR (Bosman et al.
2018; Eilers et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2020; Bosman et al. 2022;
Meyer et al. 2025). Although inhomogeneous, the global evolution of reionization can be summarized with the volume
filling fraction of ionized hydrogen QHII (z) and its counterpart, the fraction of neutral hydrogen xHI . There have been
significant efforts to constrain xHI and its redshift evolution,
both from single bright objects that can probe the GunnPeterson trough, such as quasars, and from the redshift evolution of galaxy populations. Constraints on xHI have been
placed from observations of the Lyman-α and Lyman-β absorption in the spectra of quasars (Eilers et al. 2018; Kulkarni et al.
2019; Yang et al. 2020; Bosman et al. 2022; Qin et al. 2025) and
galaxies (Meyer et al. 2025), the clustering of Lyman-α emitters
(Sobacchi & Mesinger 2015; Ouchi et al. 2018), the Lyman-α
equivalent width and luminosity function evolution (Ouchi et al.
2010; Konno et al. 2014, 2018; Zheng et al. 2017; Inoue et al.
2018; Mason et al. 2018, 2019a; Hoag et al. 2019; Whitler et al.
2020; Goto et al. 2021; Morales et al. 2021; Bolan et al. 2022;

Ning et al. 2022; Bruton et al. 2023; Morishita et al. 2023;
Nakane et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2024; Kageura et al. 2025) as
well as the damping wing of either Lyman-break galaxies

In its most basic form, the redshift evolution of the fraction
of neutral hydrogen is calculated as the balance between the recombination time scale and the rate of ionization (Madau et al.
1999). The number of ionizing photons that reach the IGM per
unit time and unit volume, the cosmic ionizing emissivity, is described as
ṅion = ρUV fesc ξion .

(1)

Here ρUV is the UV luminosity density (in units
erg s−1 Hz−1 Mpc−3 ), derived from the UV luminosity function,
fesc is the average escape fraction of ionizing photons of the
general galaxy population and ξion is the average ionizing
photon production efficiency (in units Hz erg−1 ), measuring
the amount of ionizing photons created per 1500 Å luminosity
density.
Since the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope
(JWST, Gardner et al. 2023) we have gained an unprecedented view of the rest-frame optical properties of galaxies
in the EoR, both thanks to the Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam, Rieke et al. 2023) and the Near-Infrared Spectrograph
(NIRSpec Jakobsen et al. 2022). In particular, many studies
have focused on separately measuring the three parameters
that make up our understanding of the ionizing photon output of the Universe, ρUV (Bouwens et al. 2023; Donnan et al.
2024; Harikane et al. 2025; Whitler et al. 2025; Atek et al. 2026;
Chemerynska et al. 2026; Kreilgaard et al. 2026; Weibel et al.
2026), ξion (Simmonds et al. 2024a,b; Llerena et al. 2025;
Pahl et al. 2026), and fesc (Witten et al. 2023; Mascia et al.
2024b; Saxena et al. 2024; Giovinazzo et al. 2026; Stoffers et al.
2026; Jecmen et al. 2026). However, considering each component individually does not account for possible correlations
between the parameters, which can have significant implications on the estimated ionizing budget (Muñoz et al. 2024;
Bosman & Davies 2024). In particular fesc is an exceedingly
challenging quantity to measure as effectively all ionizing photons are absorbed by the IGM in the EoR, making direct measurements impossible. A complete analysis where all the parameters are modeled together, self-consistently, is therefore necessary to fully understand the reionization process.
Giovinazzo et al. (2026) estimated fesc for a sample of
sources with NIRSpec/PRISM spectroscopy in the EoR, from
Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting. In this work, we will
expand on this analysis to estimate the rate of ionizing photon production (Ṅion,int ) for the same sample, again using SEDmodelling. As a result of the fesc prescription already implemented by Giovinazzo et al. (2026), we can infer posterior distributions for the ionizing photon emission rate (Ṅion,esc ). Similar efforts in estimating the ionizing emissivity from SED fitting
have also been undertaken by Simmonds et al. (2024b), with a
mass complete sample, although without accounting for fesc . By
estimating the ionizing photon emission rate directly through integration of the ionizing part of the spectral SED fits, we avoid
having to disentangle the separate contributions of the production efficiency and the escape fraction, along with their associated uncertainties, to Ṅion . This yields a self-consistent estimate
of the rate at which ionizing photons reach the IGM from a given
Article number, page 2

galaxy, a quantity necessary to constrain the reionization process.
In this paper, we will determine the evolution of the ionizing emissivity function of the general galaxy population as well
as the cosmic ionizing emissivity. We will then calculate the redshift evolution of the neutral hydrogen fraction with cosmic time,
as well as the contribution of strong leakers, here defined as fesc
> 10%. We will test which population of galaxies contributes
most to the ionizing emissivity, whether the few highly leaking
galaxies or the majority of weakly leaking sources. We will also
determine whether any galaxy population, i.e. at faint, intermediate or bright luminosities, is a dominant contributor of ionizing
photons to the IGM.
In Sec. 2 we introduce the sources included in our sample,
present the method that we used to calculate Ṅion and compare it
to the method typically used in the literature. In Sec. 3 we present
the ionizing properties of the sources in our sample and their redshift and magnitude evolution. In Sec. 4 we present the ionizing
emissivity function, quantifying the number of sources in a unit
volume with a given rate of ionizing photon output. In Sec. 5 we
present the evolution of the fraction of neutral hydrogen. Finally,
in Sec. 6 and Sec. 7 we present our caveats and conclusions.
Throughout this work, we assume a flat ΛCDM cosmology
with H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1 , Ωm = 0.3, and ΩΛ = 0.7. Magnitudes are given in the AB system (Oke & Gunn 1983).

2. Ionizing output calculation
2.1. Spectroscopic data

For this study, we used the data set previously presented in
Giovinazzo et al. (2026). This consists of 1428 sources with
JWST NIRSpec/PRISM observations at 5 < zspec < 10 taken
from the DAWN JWST Archive (DJA1 ) (Heintz et al. 2025;
Brammer & Valentino 2025), an online repository containing
spectroscopic data from public JWST programs, all uniformly
extracted and reduced with the same pipeline based on grizli2
and MSAexp3 . Further details on the data reduction and processing can be found in de Graaff et al. (2025); Heintz et al. (2025);
Valentino et al. (2025); Pollock et al. (2025). The database query
was performed on the 7th of February 2025 and includes all
sources in the redshift range of interest with grade=3 (i.e., with
robust redshift), MUV < −18 and excludes known Little Red
Dots (LRDs) as reported by Kocevski et al. (2025). This selection includes spectra from RUBIES (GO 4233, de Graaff et al.
2025) JADES (GTO-1180, 1181, 1210, 1286, 1287; GO3215 Eisenstein et al. 2023) GTO WIDE (GTO-1211, 1212,
1213, 1214, 1215; Maseda et al. 2024), CEERS (ERS-1345;
Finkelstein et al. 2023), UNCOVER (GO-2561, Bezanson et al.
2024), CAPERS (GO-6368; Donnan et al. 2025), GO-1433
(Hsiao et al. 2024), GO-2198 (Barrufet et al. 2025), GO-2565
(Nanayakkara et al. 2025), DD 2750 (Arrabal Haro et al. 2023),
GO-3073 (Castellano et al. 2024), DD-2756 (Mascia et al.
2024b), DD-6541 (DeCoursey et al. 2025), DD-6585 (PI: Coulter) and GO-4106 (PI: Nelson).

For each source we perform spectral SED fitting to the NIRSpec/prism data to recover the physical properties, as described

We modeled the star formation history with the continuity
prior (Leja et al. 2019), a non-parametric prior, allowing for
greater flexibility than parametric models. We modeled the star
formation rate (SFR) in seven bins –with 5, 10, 50, 100, 200,
400 and 800 Myr– unless the age of the Universe at the source’s
redshift was less than 800 Myr. In that case, the last bin ended
at the age of the Universe. With the continuity prior, bagpipes
fits for the ∆SFR between adjacent bins, which therefore adds
a number of free parameters equal to the number of bins minus
one. We used a Student-t prior with ∆SFR ∈ [−3, 3] and adopted
ν = 2 and σ = 0.3 following Leja et al. (2019). For fesc we opted
for a logarithmic prior with range 0.001 < fesc < 1. This prior
was chosen since observations (Kreilgaard et al. 2024) as well
as simulations (Choustikov et al. 2024; Yeh et al. 2023) suggest
that most sources have little-to-no leakage. This was confirmed
in Giovinazzo et al. (2026). We used the BPASS v2.2.1 stellarpopulation models (Stanway & Eldridge 2018) with the default
broken-power-law initial mass function (IMF), with slopes of
α1 = −1.30 for the 0.1 − 0.5M⊙ mass range and α2 = −2.35
for the 0.5 − 300M⊙ mass range, based on Kroupa et al. (1993).
Metallicity was modeled with a logarithmic prior, in the range
0.01 < Z/Z⊙ < 0.5. The stellar and nebular attenuation were
modeled with the Calzetti dust law (Calzetti et al. 2000), with a
linear prior, allowing for a maximum AV = 0.5 mag as we were
mainly interested in blue sources. We note that for our picketfence model dust is not applied to the free channels. For more
details on the SED fits we refer the reader to Giovinazzo et al.
(2026).
The SED fits are used to calculate the ionizing photon production for each source, as discussed in Sec. 2.4. This is then
compared to the more standard approach, presented in Sec. 2.3,
of measuring the ionizing photon production from the Hα line.
2.3. Ṅion estimation from Hα

In the standard approach used in the literature, the production
rate of ionizing photons in the EoR is estimated from the luminosity of the Hα line (e.g. Bouwens et al. 2016; Llerena et al.
2025; Pahl et al. 2026). As a recombination line, the Hα luminosity directly depends on the amount of reprocessed ionizing
photons and therefore effectively counts the number of ionizing photons that do not escape. For all the sources in our sample with Hα coverage in the PRISM wavelength range, the Hα
fluxes are extracted following Covelo-Paz et al. (2026), i.e. by
fitting a Gaussian directly to the spectrum. The luminosities are
then corrected for dust attenuation following the Calzetti et al.
(2000) dust model with attenuation AV derived from SED fitting, as LHα,int = LHα,obs × 100.4AHα .
The relation between the intrinsic Balmer line luminosity
and the total Lyman Continuum luminosity was derived from
stellar models by Leitherer & Heckman (1995). For Hα it can be
expressed as
L(Hα)[erg s−1 ] = 1.36 × 10−12 N(H0 )[s−1 ]

(2)

where N(H0 ) is the production rate of ionizing photons assuming
fesc = 0, and L(Hα) is the intrinsic Hα luminosity, which is thus

dust-corrected. This effectively assumes that all the ionizing photons are reprocessed in the Hα line and none escape. Hence, the
ionizing photon production that we find from this method must
be corrected for the escape fraction. As seen in Giovinazzo et al.
(2026), most sources have very low escape fraction, making this
a good approximation. There is, however, a small fraction of
sources with very high escape fraction, as the fesc distribution
approximates an exponential with a mean of 10%. The ionizing
photon production rate is then given by

2.5. Comparison of ionizing fluxes

The two methods described before are complementary in how
they count the amount of ionizing photons, as the Hα method
counts the rate of photons that are reprocessed into nebular continuum, while the SED integration directly counts the rate at
which ionizing photons escape. Because of this, the only way
to consistently compare these two methods is by using Ṅion,int ,
the intrinsic rate of ionizing photons production.

used in the literature (e.g., Bouwens et al. 2016; Llerena et al.
2025; Pahl et al. 2026). We find the Hα estimates to be consistently lower, with a median difference between the estimates of ∆ = 0.05 dex, with only few sources lying significantly above or below the 1:1 line. This small discrepancy is
likely due to the assumptions in the N(H0 ) conversion to L(Hα)

3. Ionizing properties
3.1. Redshift evolution

Simmonds et al. (2024b) have shown that Ṅion evolves with redshift when considering a spectroscopic-only sample, with higher
ionizing emissivity at higher redshift. However, they have also
demonstrated that this redshift evolution disappears when using
a mass complete sample. This suggests a bias of the spectroscopic sample toward star forming galaxies rather than a real
change in the ionizing emissivity with redshift for the global
galaxy population. An important caveat of this previous analysis
is the lack of a prescription for fesc and therefore the assumption
of fesc = 0 for the calculation of Ṅion . We investigate the possibility of a redshift evolution of Ṅion , both intrinsic and escaped,
in our sample, which includes a prescription for fesc .
We find that the intrinsic ionizing photon production rate
Ṅion,int shows a shallow evolution with redshift, as seen in the
top panel of Fig. 2. This trend may be due to a selection bias,
as discussed by Simmonds et al. (2024b). It may also be due to
a redshift evolution of MUV or fesc which would translate to a
redshift evolution in Ṅion , the latter of which would have been
missed in previous studies. In Giovinazzo et al. (2026), we do in
fact see a shallow trend between redshift and fesc for our sources,
as also shown in Ferrara et al. (2025).

3.2. UV magnitude dependence

We additionally explore the redshift evolution of Ṅion,esc , as
previously performed for the intrinsic value. We choose to use
two linear fits to distinguish the population of strong-leakers
( fesc > 10%) from that of weak-leakers ( fesc < 10%). We choose
to split the sample at fesc = 10% as this is the mean fesc of
the entire sample, as shown in Giovinazzo et al. (2026). While
both populations show a shallow redshift evolution, it is stronger
for the high fesc population, although the evolution is consistent
within the uncertainties. This redshift evolution is closer to that
of the intrinsic production rate, with a slope of 0.09 ± 0.02, as
most ionizing photons that are created are also emitted in the
case of high fesc . Instead, the low fesc population shows a shallower distribution, with a slope of 0.05 ± 0.02, about 1.5 dex
below the strong leakers. As mentioned for Ṅion,intr this redshift
evolution could be due to a selection bias, or to a redshift evolution of MUV or fesc (e.g. Mascia et al. 2024a; Cain et al. 2025a;
Ferrara et al. 2025). This would need to be studied further with
mass-complete samples in the future. As described below, we
will ignore any redshift evolution for the further analysis.

In Fig. 3 we investigate the relation between Ṅion,esc and UV
magnitude. The ionizing photon production rate is proportional,
to first order, to the rate of non-ionizing UV photon production. Hence, we expect Ṅion,int to be proportional to the intrinsic UV luminosity. Indeed, it is immediately visible that Ṅion,esc
increases towards brighter MUV . This allows us to investigate
the impact of the escape mechanism on this relation. We color
code the sample by fesc , highlighting the same behavior already
seen in Fig. 2. All of the low fesc sources are clustered around
log(Ṅion,esc /s−1 ) ∼ 52, while the fesc ≳ 10% sources span the
range 53 ≲ log(Ṅion,esc /s−1 ) ≲ 55, on average about 1.5 dex
above the cloud of low fesc sources. As mentioned above, however, the Ṅion values are artificially limited in our fitting because
of the limit on fesc .
Overall, from Fig. 3 it remains clear that galaxies with the
highest escape fractions also emit the most ionizing photons into
the IGM, in absolute number. Indeed, above log(Ṅion,esc [s−1 ]) >
53 only 8% of sources have an fesc < 10%. While faint galaxies
with fesc ∼ 50% may emit as many ionizing photons as bright
galaxies with only fesc ∼ 10%, this result shows that fesc is the
key quantity to determine the sources of reionization, as it is the
main factor controlling which sources leak a significant amount
of ionizing photons. Indeed, even for very UV-bright sources that
intrinsically produce a lot of ionizing photons, a very low escape
fraction means that a negligible amount of them reach the IGM,
even compared to fainter sources with an increased fesc .

5.1. Contributions to the neutral gas fraction

One of the biggest open questions in the study of the EoR is understanding which kind of sources contribute most to the reionization process. We try to answer this question by quantifying the
contribution of strong leakers to the evolution of the neutral hydrogen fraction over the history of the Universe. This will tell us
whether it is the few highly leaking sources or the many weakly
leaking sources that drive the bulk of the reionization process.
Similarly to what was already done in Fig. 6 we split our sampling between sources with log(ξion fesc /Hz erg−1 ) < 24.5 and
those above that value and calculate what the evolution of the
neutral hydrogen fraction for each sample (see Fig 9). We find
that reionization is almost entirely driven by the few sources with
a high escape fraction. These sources alone can ionize the universe in agreement with the observational constraints. The majority of sources, those with fesc ≲ 0.1, do not have a significant contribution to the ionization of neutral gas in the universe.
This shows that it is in fact the few sources with very high escape fraction, ∼ 20% of the total sample, that perform the bulk
of reionization, producing ∼ 87% of all ionizing photons in the
EoR, instead of the majority of sources with low escape fraction. This is broadly in agreement with recent results from the
PRISMS survey, who also find that a minority of sources with
very high fesc should be responsible for the majority of the reionization process (Marques-Chaves et al. 2026). It should be noted
that high fesc is likely a temporal event. Simulations in fact show
that fesc can drastically change in galaxies as a function of time,
usually following star formation events (Trebitsch et al. 2017).
It would therefore not be the same 20% of sources driving all of
the reionization process but nonetheless, at any given time, we
expect that only a minority of sources strongly contribute to this
process.

6. Caveats
Here we would like to discuss the main assumptions and caveats
of this work.
Article number, page 10

First of all we would like to once again note, as already discussed in Giovinazzo et al. (2026), the degeneracy between star
formation history and escape fraction, as this would affect our
estimates of Ṅion,esc . However, we had already found that this uncertainty is less important for very high fesc sources, which often
have spectra that cannot be reproduced with fesc = 0. Therefore,
as we have found that the sources with fesc > 10% are the main
drivers of reionization, we do not think that this degeneracy will
have a strong impact on our results. Moreover, there is a category of sources, remnant leakers (Katz et al. 2023), for which
this degeneracy is not important, as they are quenched sources
with nonetheless high fesc . These sources would have still young
stellar populations but little ISM, making them efficient leakers.
We would also like to note that since our method to calculate Ṅion,esc relies on SED fitting, it includes assumptions on the
stellar populations, initial mass functions (IMFs) and dust attenuation laws. We assume a BPASS v2.2.1 stellar population
(Stanway & Eldridge 2018) with the default broken power law
IMF, so if these assumptions were to not be accurate it could
affect our results. In this context, our quantitative conclusions
should be interpreted within our modeling framework, although
we do not believe that our qualitative conclusions on the sources
of reionization would be affected.
Possibly the biggest caveat of the analysis presented in this
work is the assumption of a lack of magnitude evolution for
log(ξion fesc ). This assumption allows us to expand the analysis
to magnitudes not covered by our initial sample and investigate
the impact of the faintest galaxies to reionization. However, just
because we find no evidence of a trend between log(ξion fesc ) and
MUV in our sample it does not necessarily mean that this will be
true at all magnitudes. Overcoming this would require an analysis of the ionizing output of sources covering the fainter end of
the magnitude distribution that we are lacking here. Moreover,
due to our choice to use only spectra with a confident redshift
estimate, we might be biasing our sample to only strong line
emitters, which would also bias our log(ξion fesc ) distribution.
Lastly, we would like to mention the issue of cosmic variance. As we have seen that the estimate of the neutral hydrogen fraction is very sensitive to the UV luminosity function uncertainty, especially in the faint end slope, we have chosen to
use the luminosity function from GLIMPSE (Chemerynska et al.
2026; Atek et al. 2026). However, GLIMPSE is only one NIRCam pointing of a lensed cluster, which significantly reduces the
effective volume. It could therefore be highly affected by cosmic variance, although this effect is less problematic for very
faint sources. Alleviating this issue would require many more
extremely deep field observations with JWST or the upcoming
Extremely Large Telescope (ELT).

7. Conclusion
In this work we leverage JWST/NIRSpec spectra from a variety
of public observations in the redshift range 5 < z < 10, retrieved
from the DJA, to compile a sample of 1428 sources. Using the
SED fits already performed in Giovinazzo et al. (2026) we investigate the ionizing photon production of galaxies in the Epoch of
Reionization. We estimate the ionizing photon output of individual galaxies, accounting for their intrinsic ionizing photon production and their escape fraction. Starting from the GLIMPSE
UV luminosity functions (Chemerynska et al. 2026; Atek et al.
2026), as a way to account for the incompleteness of our sample,
we also compute the ionizing emissivity function. This quantifies
the amount of sources emitting a given rate of ionizing photons
in the IGM per unit time per unit volume. We also determine the

– From our SED fits we calculate the amount of ionizing photon produced by each galaxy, as well as the amount of ionizing photons emitted into the IGM, when the escape fraction
is taken into account. We find that the intrinsic production
shows a slight evolution towards higher values at higher redshifts. This is consistent with the results of Simmonds et al.
(2024b), who also use a spectroscopic only sample, possibly
indicating that this trend is due to the incompleteness and selection function of the sample. We find a similar trend for the
escaped rate of ionizing photons, which also increases as a
function of redshift, although at a different rate for fesc < 0.1
and fesc > 0.1. Indeed we find a stronger evolution, more
consistent with the intrinsic, for strong leakers, while weak
leakers show an almost flat trend.
– We find that in general the sources with higher fesc are in
fact those that emit the most ionizing photons into the IGM,
which is an expected although not obvious result. At a constant UV magnitude we find a cloud of low fesc sources about
∼ 1.5 dex below the leakers. This translates into the presence
of two clouds, one of strong leakers and one of weak leakers
also in log10 (ξion fesc ).
– The ionizing emissivity function decreases with increasing
redshift, following the behavior of the UV luminosity function. We study the behavior of this function when splitting
the sample at log10 (ξion fesc ) = 24.5, roughly corresponding
to fesc ∼ 0.1 split and the mean of the sample. In this case we
find that the main contribution to the rate of ionizing photons
emitted into the IGM per unit volume comes almost entirely
from sources above these values. Only at low Ṅion values low
fesc sources start contributing.
– Based on the ionizing emissivity function we estimate the
evolution of the fraction of neutral hydrogen with redshift.

We find its evolution to be consistent with literature estimates and that the uncertainty on the UV luminosity function is the lead cause of uncertainties in our estimates, especially the uncertainty in the faint end slope. We compare
this evolution to that found when assuming fesc = 0.1 and
log(ξion / Hz erg−1 ) = 25.45 as motivated from our results.
We find them to be in broad agreement with the latter being
faster. We also estimate τCMB and find it in agreement with
the results from Planck Collaboration et al. (2020).
– We determine the contribution to the ionized hydrogen fraction by strong leakers by performing again the same analysis over two samples split at log(ξion fesc / Hz erg−1 ) = 24.5.
We find that the strong leakers ( fesc > 0.1) alone are almost
able to match the reionization history that we find for all the
sources. This shows that the few strongly leaking sources,
∼20% of the total sources in the universe, are able to entirely
drive the reionization process, producing ∼ 87% of all ionizing photons that reach the IGM. This indicates that it is necessary to identify highly leaking galaxies to find the drivers
of reionization.
– Lastly, we study the contribution to the total ṅion from
sources of different magnitudes. We find that at high redshift sources with MUV > −18 all seem to contribute equally,
while at low redshift bright sources (MUV ∼ −20) contribute
most.
With this work we have presented a framework to calculate
the ionizing photon emission of individual galaxies, as well as
the rate of ionizing photons being emitted in a unit volume. Our
results are consistent with observational constraints and show
that 20% of galaxies with high fesc are almost entirely responsible for reionization, rather than the majority of low ionizing output galaxies. The relative contributions of faint and bright galaxies vary with redshift, with bright sources dominating at the end
of reionization and faint sources at its onset. Pinning down the
drivers of reionization will ultimately require pushing fesc measurements to the faint, abundant galaxies that our spectroscopic
